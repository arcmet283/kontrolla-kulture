Every coaching staff has a story about how it ended up where it did. This is the long version of his — a playing career that ended quietly, eleven years of being somebody else's assistant across three franchises, two head-job interviews that went nowhere, and the program that finally handed him the keys. None of it is about what's happened on the field since. That part lives in the season recaps. This is just how he got to the building in the first place.

SEGMENT 01

A Player First

Head Coach · Southern Jaguars · Hired 2025

Teddy Graves

Before any of the sidelines, there was a linebacker who never quite had the explosiveness to match his instincts. Four years as a backup and spot starter at a mid-major program that didn't produce many NFL names, the kind of career that ends with a diploma and a box of game tape nobody else is ever going to watch. He has said, more than once, that the moment he knew he'd coach for a living wasn't a highlight — it was a film session his senior year where he correctly diagnosed an opposing offense's tendencies three snaps before the position coach did, out loud, to a room that went quiet.

That's the whole origin story, by his own telling: not good enough to play on Sundays, apparently good enough to see the game a half-step ahead of people who were. He took a graduate assistant job the year after he stopped playing and never left the profession.

SEGMENT 02

The Long Climb: Three Franchises, Eleven Years

Eleven years on NFL sidelines, three different organizations, three different title changes — and across all of it, never once the title he actually wanted. The shape of the climb is worth laying out in full, because it's the whole reason he talks about Southern the way he does.

Yrs 1–4Quality control assistant, Cleveland Browns. Breaking down opponent film at odd hours, building the reports the position coaches actually got credit for using. The job that exists specifically so nobody has to ask the head coach a question he doesn't want to answer in the moment.
Yrs 5–9Defensive backs coach, Baltimore Ravens. First real seat at the table — his own room, his own players, his own game-week install. Five years in the same seat is long enough to either get a coordinator job or get passed over for one more than once. He got passed over.
Yrs 10–11Special teams coordinator, Jacksonville Jaguars. The unit every fan ignores until it loses a game by itself. He has called it "the only coordinator job in football where you're already expected to apologize before the season starts" — and also the job that finally put "coordinator" on his resume, which is what got him interviews at all. Southern's own staff has never let him live down that his last stop before college ball was literally the Jaguars.

Three different organizations across those eleven years, which means three different coordinators' systems absorbed, three different staffs' politics survived, and by his own account, three different philosophies he picked apart piece by piece and kept only what actually worked. Nothing about that climb was fast, and nothing about it was handed to him.

SEGMENT 03

Two Doors That Didn't Open

He interviewed for two head-coaching jobs before Southern ever called, and didn't get either. The first was Troy, early in his special-teams-coordinator years, when he was still a long shot on paper and knew it going in. The feedback that made it back to him was almost encouraging in how specific it was: good coach, respected in the building, no track record of actually running one. It's the exact catch-22 every career assistant eventually runs into — you can't get the record without the job, and you can't get the job without the record.

The second was James Madison, two years later, and that's the one he's described as the harder loss — not because it stung more in the moment, but because it was the interview where he walked out certain he'd done everything right and still didn't get a call back. That's the version of the story that explains, more than anything else, why he answered the way he did when Southern finally asked.

SEGMENT 04

The Call From Southern

Southern wasn't hiring for a name, and everyone involved in the search knew it. The program was picked to finish at the bottom of a crowded conference, with a roster that had been passed over by better-funded schools for years and a budget that wasn't going to change that fact on its own. What the administration wanted was someone who'd coached under real pressure without the ego that usually comes with it — someone who'd spent over a decade being told what to do well enough to know exactly what he'd do differently with the final say.

Graves fit that search in a way a bigger name never would have. No head-coaching record meant no outside expectations to manage on day one. Eleven years without his own program meant he'd had a long time to sit with exactly how he'd run one, if anybody ever let him. The interview, by accounts from people in the room, wasn't really a job interview at all by the end — it turned into him describing, in detail, a plan for a program that didn't exist yet.

SEGMENT 05

Walking Into The Building

The roster he inherited wasn't close to what he'd coached in the NFL, and he's never pretended otherwise. What he found on his first walk through the facility, by his own description, was a program that had quietly accepted its own ceiling — good enough attitudes, no shortage of effort, but no real infrastructure behind any of it. No analytics department to speak of. A recruiting board that read like a list of players other schools had already said no to.

He's said the single biggest adjustment wasn't schematic, it was cultural: getting a locker room that had been losing for years to believe a plan existed at all, let alone trust it enough to run it full speed in front of 16,000 people on a Saturday.

SEGMENT 06

Building His Own Staff

His first hires weren't splashy, and that was deliberate. He went after other career assistants — people he'd crossed paths with at previous stops, position coaches who'd been passed over for coordinator jobs the same way he'd been passed over for head jobs. The pitch to each of them was close to identical to the one Southern had given him: no name recognition, no budget advantage, a real chance to build something and get credit for it this time.

It's a staff built almost entirely out of people with something to prove, assembled by a head coach who, eleven months earlier, was still trying to prove the exact same thing himself.

SEGMENT 07

The Plan He Wrote Down

He said it out loud the day he took the job, and it's been repeated in team meetings often enough that new hires reportedly hear it before they've unpacked their office: "We're not out-recruiting anybody here. We're going to out-work them." That line is spray-painted on a wall inside the team's weight room today. It was never meant as a slogan for a press release — by every account from inside the building, it's the literal operating plan.

The plan underneath the plan is specific, not vague: establish the run on offense, build a defense that makes teams beat it through the air to win, and fill the roster with players other programs had already passed on rather than chase recruits Southern's budget was never going to land anyway. It's a scheme built around what he actually had to work with, not what he wished he had — the kind of plan eleven years of watching other people's rosters get built teaches a coach to make.

"We're not out-recruiting anybody here. We're going to out-work them."
SEGMENT 08

The Man Behind It

Away from the building, people who know him describe someone almost deliberately unremarkable — no social-media presence to speak of, no catchphrases built for a highlight reel, a coach who still calls special-teams coverages himself out of pure habit from his coordinator years even though he has a full staff to do it now. He keeps a single framed photo in his office, not a trophy or a highlight, but the picture from his introductory press conference: roster sheet in hand, looking at a depth chart with more question marks on it than answers.

He shows up before the sun most mornings and expects the same from everyone on staff, a habit that traces straight back to the quality-control years, when being the first person in the building was the only way anybody ever noticed the work.

SEGMENT 09

What He Told The Team

His first team meeting, by multiple accounts, ran shorter than anyone expected and covered almost nothing about scheme. He introduced his staff, laid out the two things he said would never change no matter how a season went — the run-first offense and the bend-but-don't-break identity on defense — and then told a room full of players who'd been through a coaching change before that he wasn't interested in promises. He was interested in whether they'd still be in the building at six in the morning a month later. By his own account, most of them were.

How the plan has actually played out on the field, season by season, is its own story. The full accounting of that is in each year's season recap.
